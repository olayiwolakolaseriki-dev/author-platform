The Borrowed Map

Navigating Faith, Ambition, and the Foreign Scripts of Modern Africa

For generations, the roadmap of African ambition has been shaped by foreign contours—from colonial administrative curricula to Western corporate archetypes and imported religious systems.

In The Borrowed Map, Olayiwola Kola-Seriki traces the profound disorientation that occurs when external validation becomes the sole barometer of personal and societal progress, laying out a rigorous call to rediscover our own coordinates.

“The map you inherited is not the territory. Draw your own.”

The Central Question

“Whose map are you holding?”

When I was four years old in Western Nigeria, my family called me “Doctor Layi.” I had never seen an operating theatre, but I understood the unspoken code: give the answer that appeals to the community. Three decades later, I had followed an inherited syllabus to impressive effect—an accounting degree, an MBA, CITN Fellowship, ANAN qualifications, and an executive seat as Chief Financial Officer in Nigeria’s indigenous fintech sector.

Yet at Charles de Gaulle Airport, confronted with the familiar discomfort of being African at a European border, I found myself asking a question I could no longer ignore: had I built a life of my own, or simply become very good at following a map someone else had drawn?

The Borrowed Map examines how modern African societies quietly train us to distrust our own judgment. It traces the habits formed in childhood—the wooden vernacular token used to shame students caught speaking their mother tongue, and a culture of elder deference prizing silence over inquiry. Under economic strain, the pressure to conform intensified: as institutions decayed, families pursued credentialism and religious promises of sudden breakthrough, seeking escape rather than building structural competence.

We surrendered our personal compasses to a narrow, anxious triad: Doctor, Lawyer, or Japa. We treated emigration as our only salvation and carried the heavy burden of Black Tax, assuming the next stamp in an expatriate passport would resolve our displacement.

Part memoir, part cultural critique, and part leadership reflection, The Borrowed Map refuses simplistic slogans. Moving from schoolyards to executive boardrooms, and ending on an Osogbo ridge where a surveyor’s drone maps ancestral soil under my daughter Simi’s gaze, this book asks the defining question for modern Africa: when inherited maps lead us into systemic drift, do we finally have the courage to draw our own?

Author's Perspective

Why I Wrote The Borrowed Map

I wrote this book because I watched some of the most gifted minds of my generation achieve everything our parents, schools, and foreign institutions demanded of them—only to arrive at a profound spiritual exhaustion.

We had mastered the curricula. We had learned to translate our ideas into the acceptable vocabularies of Western boardrooms, think tanks, and academic faculties. But in doing so, we had silently accepted an unspoken premise: that to become significant, we had to leave ourselves behind.

The Borrowed Map is not an exercise in bitterness or isolationism. It is a work of gratitude for the journey, coupled with the courage to say: the map we were handed is insufficient for where we must go next. It is written for anyone who has ever felt the silent dislocation of succeeding in a system that was never designed with their spiritual and cultural flourishing in mind.

— Olayiwola “Layi” Kola-Seriki

The Borrowed Map · Manuscript Excerpt

Prologue: The Cartographers of Our Minds

I. The Traveler at Terminal 2E · Completed Manuscript (39,387 Words)

Watch an African professional standing in the immigration queue at Paris’s Charles de Gaulle Airport Terminal 2E on a cold, misty Tuesday morning, and you will witness a subtle, involuntary metamorphosis.

Before the Air France flight touched down on the tarmac of Roissy, this man was a giant in his world. In Lagos or Abuja, he is an accomplished senior financial controller and corporate strategist. He commands the respect of his community, and speaks with booming, rhythmic authority in both English and his native tongue.

Yet, as the queue slowly snakes toward the glass booths of the French border police (Police aux Frontières), his posture undergoes a quiet, defensive contraction. He checks his leather travel wallet for the fifth time. He adjusts his woolen overcoat—bought specifically for European winter board meetings—patting down the neat stack of supporting documents: the hotel reservation in the 8th arrondissement, the return ticket, the corporate invitation letter with its embossed seal, the bank balance statements certified with bank stamps, and his Schengen business visa. His breathing turns shallow. Under his breath, without consciously realizing it, his tone shifts, softening its hard African consonants, smoothing out vowels to sound non-threatening, polished, and impeccably deferential.

When he finally steps forward to face a twenty-four-year-old French border officer in a dark blue uniform, he offers a tight, practiced smile. For sixty seconds, the entirety of his life, his intellect, his accomplishments, and his dignity are held hostage by the silent movement of a rubber stamp hovering over a blank passport page.

Now, follow that same man on his return flight two weeks later as he steps off the plane into the heavy, humid heat of Murtala Muhammed International Airport in Lagos. The transformation reverses itself with violent speed. As soon as he clears the jet bridge, the deferential posture vanishes. His shoulders square back. His voice drops into a commanding register. As baggage handlers and security officials circle with outstretched hands and hungry eyes, he deploys the defensive status markers of the African elite: he snaps his fingers, drops the name of a federal director, brandishes his European luggage tags, and barks down a customs inspector who dares to question his briefcase.

In Paris, he was terrified of being treated as an illegal migrant; in Lagos, he is terrified of being treated as an ordinary citizen. In both airports, separated by six hours of flight and four thousand kilometers of open sky, this man is not living as a free, centered human being. He is an actor trapped in a perpetual identity whiplash, frantically switching between two different sets of borrowed masks.

He is navigating his life using a map that was never drawn for him. I know this man intimately. For nearly two decades, whenever I caught my reflection in the polished glass of those airport terminals, the face staring back was my own.
